Drift and Distance: When Is Score Centering Enough in Async RL?

October 27 · 2026

In asynchronous RL for LLMs, the sampler generates responses while the trainer updates the policy. The trainer therefore learns from data produced by an older policy, a gap called staleness. Even without staleness, the two sides can differ. Setup bugs, such as a mismatched temperature, top-p, top-k, MoE routing, or tokenization, can make training collapse, but each is easy to fix (Fang & Khazi, 2025). Different kernels and a lower-precision sampler always leave a small mismatch (Yao et al., 2025).

Score centering (SC) removes the most harmful part of this error, a systematic push that we call drift, which amplifies itself (Marek & Ryabinin, 2026). Do we need anything beyond SC? The SC paper pairs SC with truncated importance sampling (TIS) and trains stably. Zhang (2026) finds that the same combination collapses and adds a KL penalty instead, and Thinking Machines' StableReinforce also pairs SC with a KL penalty (Marek, 2026). Why does the same recipe work in one setup and fail in another? We find that the answer lies in distance, how far the trainer moves from the sampler before fresh data arrives. What SC leaves depends on this distance, and batch size, learning rate, and staleness control it.

Our findings

  • Score centering removes the most dangerous error. Without it, training collapses at batch 64 and staleness 15 with no importance sampling, vanilla IS, or TIS, each for its own reason. SC alone trains to the end.
  • What SC leaves is distance, and part of it is learning itself. The KL between the trainer and the sampler has a noise part and a signal part. A larger batch averages away the noise, so the trainer does not move away from the sampler by chance. The signal part is learning. On old data, the trainer keeps improving until it is several versions ahead of the policy that wrote the data, and those samples no longer describe its own policy. No batch size removes this part. Only less staleness or a lower learning rate shrinks it.
  • Extra losses on top of SC help less than expected. TIS keeps the damping on tokens the trainer is pushing down, which slows how fast it moves away from the sampler. But truncation also removes the correction carried by rare tokens with large weights, so TIS cannot stop the sudden KL spikes that end training. A KL penalty prevents collapse, but at best it matches simply lowering the learning rate, and with Tinker’s default strength a 4B run barely learns.

1. Background and setup

This section introduces the two errors of off-policy data and the corrections that target them, then describes how we test when SC alone is enough.

1.1 Two errors

Off-policy data introduces two kinds of error (Marek & Ryabinin, 2026; Zhang, 2026), shown in Figure 1. Below, \(p\) is the trainer's policy and \(q\) the sampler's.

Drift. Under on-policy training, the expected policy gradient contains only the learning signal: it raises the probability of high-advantage tokens. When the data comes from the sampler instead, the expected gradient picks up an extra term that does not depend on which token is better. Under a softmax parameterization, this term points along \(q - p\), scaled by the mean advantage at the prefix.

Derivation: where drift comes from

Fix a prefix and write the trainer's policy as a softmax over logits \(z\). With respect to the logits, \(\nabla_z \log p(x) = e_x - p\), where \(e_x\) is the one-hot vector of token \(x\). We use logit gradients here because they are easy to read. Elsewhere, \(\nabla \log p\) is the gradient with respect to the parameters, and the two differ only by the chain rule through the logits. On-policy, the score averages to zero, \(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[\nabla_z \log p(x)] = p - p = 0\), so the expected gradient \(\mathbb{E}_p[A\nabla_z \log p]\) only says which tokens are better than average.

When the data comes from the sampler \(q\), we split each advantage into the mean advantage at the prefix, \(\bar A = \mathbb{E}_q[A]\), and the rest:

\[ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\big[A\,\nabla_z \log p(x)\big] = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\big[(A-\bar A)\,e_x\big]}_{\text{learning signal}} \;+\; \underbrace{\bar A\,(q-p)}_{\text{drift}}. \]

The second term does not depend on which token is better. It points from \(p\) toward \(q\) and is scaled by \(\bar A\). Score centering subtracts \(\mathbb{E}_q[\nabla_z \log p] = q - p\) from every score, which removes exactly this term (Marek & Ryabinin, 2026).

On-policy, raising the probability of whatever was sampled averages out, so only the advantage sets the direction. Off-policy it does not average out. The trainer is nudged toward the sampler when the prefix is going well (\(\bar A > 0\)) and away from it when the prefix is going badly (\(\bar A < 0\)). In the second case, the further the trainer has moved, the larger \(q - p\) and the harder the push, which is why drift amplifies itself.

Reweighting error. The sampler draws tokens from its own distribution, but the trainer needs feedback weighted by its own. If the trainer assigns 10% to a token the sampler assigns 1%, that token appears 10× less often than the trainer needs. Weighting it by 10 would restore the balance.

Derivation: the reweighting error

The trainer wants \(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[A\nabla \log p(x)]\), but its data comes from \(q\). Importance sampling rewrites this target as an average over the sampler's data:

\[ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\big[A\,\nabla \log p(x)\big] = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\Big[\tfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,A\,\nabla \log p(x)\Big]. \]

Without the weight \(w = p/q\), each token counts with the sampler's frequency instead of the trainer's. This error grows with the KL between the two policies (Zhang, 2026). With the weight, the average is correct, but its variance grows with \(\mathbb{E}_q[w^2]\) (Section 4.2).

1.2 Three corrections

Three corrections are in common use. Score centering targets drift, importance sampling targets reweighting error, and a KL penalty keeps the trainer near the sampler.

  • Score centering (SC) removes drift without introducing weights. It recenters each token’s gradient around its average under the sampler. That average is what drift adds, so subtracting it cancels drift. The reweighting error remains.
  • Importance sampling (IS) corrects reweighting error by weighting each token by the ratio of the trainer’s probability to the sampler’s. When the two policies diverge, a few weights can become extreme (Section 4.2). The standard remedy is truncated importance sampling (TIS), which truncates the weights at a ceiling \(C\) (Yao et al., 2025).
  • A KL penalty pulls the trainer back toward the sampler with strength \(\beta\).

Figure 2 compares the options by the coefficient each puts on the drift term. Without correction, the coefficient is the mean advantage at the prefix, which can have either sign. A positive one pulls the trainer toward the sampler and stops once the gap closes. A negative one pushes the trainer away, harder the further it has gone, and this feedback makes drift the most dangerous error. SC sets the coefficient to zero, and a KL penalty on top of SC sets it to \(+\beta\), which always pulls toward the sampler (Zhang, 2026).

1.3 When is SC enough?

So far we have split off-policy error into drift and reweighting error (Section 1.1) and seen that SC removes the drift (Section 1.2). Is SC then enough on its own? The answer depends on distance: how far the trainer moves from the sampler before fresh data arrives, measured with KL. This KL bounds the reweighting error that SC leaves (Zhang, 2026). Close to the sampler, stale data is still a good guide. Far from it, SC alone breaks down.

To find where this boundary lies, we vary staleness and batch size, the two settings that differ most between the setups we started from, and compare SC alone with SC plus importance sampling or a KL penalty.

2. SC stabilizes training, but not always

We first test SC and importance sampling side by side in one setting, then make the batch larger, and then make the data older and the batch smaller.

2.1 Removing drift: SC and importance sampling, on and off

Section 1.2 described what each correction targets. Here we test them together in one setup, with batch 64 and a maximum staleness of 15. Figure 4 runs three strategies, no importance sampling (no IS), untruncated importance sampling (vanilla IS), and truncated importance sampling (TIS, ceiling \(C = 2\)), each with and without SC.

Without SC, all three runs collapse. No IS keeps all of the drift, TIS truncates part of the correction so drift returns, and vanilla IS fails from extreme weights instead (Section 4.2).

With SC, SC alone and SC + TIS train to the end. SC + vanilla IS, starred in Figure 4, collapses exactly like vanilla IS, from extreme weights rather than drift (Section 4.2). In this setting, SC alone is enough.

How the six runs differ

All six can be written in a single formula, and Section 1.1 derives where its drift term comes from. For each token, the update direction is:

\[ \hat g = A \Big( w(x)\,\nabla\log p(x) \;-\; \mathbb{E}_{x'\sim q}\big[w(x')\,\nabla\log p(x')\big] \Big), \]

where \(x\) is the sampled token, \(A\) is its advantage, and the expectation runs over all candidate tokens at that position under the sampler \(q\). The weight \(w\) depends on the IS variant: \(w = 1\) for no IS, \(w = p(x)/q(x)\) for vanilla IS (the ratio \(\pi_{\text{train}}/\pi_{\text{rollout}}\)), and \(w = \min(p(x)/q(x),\, C)\) for TIS. The second term inside the parentheses is SC, the sampler average that cancels drift (Section 1.2). Without SC, only the first term remains.

Vanilla IS removes drift in expectation. With \(w = p/q\), \(\mathbb{E}_q[w\nabla\log p] = \mathbb{E}_p[\nabla\log p] = 0\), so the SC term is already zero. This is why the starred run in Figure 4 matches vanilla IS.

2.2 Large batches: SC alone keeps up with on-policy training

Batch 64 was small enough for vanilla IS to collapse. We now raise the batch to 512 at the same staleness and ask whether that keeps all three methods stable (Figure 5).

The left two panels of Figure 5 show that a larger batch is enough to stabilize all three methods. At batch 512, they all keep up with on-policy to the end, while at batch 64 the curves are noisy and vanilla IS collapses. The next subsection shows the same pattern on 4B at batch 128.

The larger batch works by shrinking the distance. With less noise in each step, the trainer does not move away from the sampler by chance. An 8× larger batch lowers the median KL about 8×, and truncation then almost never triggers. Section 3 explains why the drop is this close to the batch ratio.

A larger batch helps only once drift is removed. Without any correction, the KL explodes within the first rollout at batch 512 just as it does at batch 64, while with SC it stays small at both (Figure 6). Drift pushes every step in the same direction, so averaging over more samples does not cancel it.

2.3 High staleness and small batches: SC alone can break

Next we move to the opposite regime, with older data and smaller batches. Unlike vanilla IS, SC does not multiply the gradient by importance weights, so a single extreme weight cannot break it. It can still fail when the trainer moves far. We run SC alone at staleness 63 (64 mini-batches per rollout) with batches of 32, 64, and 512.

The left panel of Figure 7 shows that SC alone keeps up with on-policy at batch 512 but breaks down at smaller batches. Even the batch-64 seed that survives ends 14 points below its best. In the right panel, the smaller the batch, the higher the largest KL in each rollout, about 1.5 at batch 32 against 0.1 to 0.3 at batch 512. We repeat the test on 4B with the harder DAPO-Math-17k at staleness 15, which also lets us compare the three methods.

The 4B runs in Figure 8 fail the same way at small batch. At batch 16, all three methods collapse, and each collapse starts with a sudden spike in the KL to the sampler. At batch 128, all three keep up with on-policy and roughly double the base model’s AIME scores. The differences among them are within sampling error (see the evaluation below). In both models, each failure comes with a large KL to the sampler, and Section 3 looks at where that distance comes from.

3. Why SC fails: how the trainer moves away from the sampler

SC removes drift but does not limit how far the trainer moves on stale data. To see where that distance comes from, we split the KL within a rollout into a part from noise and a part from the learning signal, then test the split by changing the batch alone.

We split the distance into noise and signal, rather than into bias and variance. In a bias–variance view, both parts are errors to trade off and minimize. Here, the signal part is learning itself. Learning fast has a price, and in async training that price shows up as distance from the sampler.

Definition. Within each rollout, after the sampler generates data, the trainer updates for \(k\) consecutive steps on that data. We measure the divergence with the per-token KL, \(\mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p_k)\), where \(q\) is the sampler's policy and \(p_k\) is the trainer's policy after \(k\) steps. We estimate it on the sampled tokens as the mean of \(w - 1 - \log w\), with \(w = p/q\) (Schulman, 2020). At the start of a rollout the KL is near zero, and it grows with each trainer step.

Signal and noise. Each step's gradient has two parts. The signal is the common direction all batches agree on. The noise is the random deviation specific to the current batch, with zero mean and variance proportional to \(1/B\). After \(k\) steps, the parameter displacement is:

\[ \theta_k - \theta_0 = -\eta\Big(\underbrace{k\,g}_{\text{signal: grows like } k} + \underbrace{\varepsilon_1 + \cdots + \varepsilon_k}_{\text{noise: grows like } \sqrt{k}}\Big). \]

Here \(\theta_0\) is the parameter at the start of the rollout, \(\theta_k\) the parameter after \(k\) steps, \(\eta\) the learning rate, \(g\) the signal (the expected gradient), and \(\varepsilon_i\) the noise at step \(i\). The signal accumulates in the same direction, so \(k\) steps give \(k\) steps of displacement. The noise is random across steps and partly cancels, so \(k\) steps give only about \(\sqrt{k}\).

KL is approximately a quadratic form of the displacement, \(\mathrm{KL} \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\theta^\top F \Delta\theta\), where \(F\) is the Fisher information matrix and measures how sensitive the policy is along each direction. Substituting the displacement and taking the expectation, the cross terms vanish and the KL splits into two terms:

\[ \mathrm{KL}_k \;\approx\; c_{\text{noise}}\,\frac{\eta^2 k}{B} \;+\; c_{\text{signal}}\,\eta^2 k^2. \]

Here \(c_{\text{signal}} = \tfrac{1}{2} g^\top F g\) is the squared length of the signal, weighted by sensitivity. Likewise, \(c_{\text{noise}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(F\Sigma)\) is the gradient variance of a single response, weighted the same way, where \(\Sigma\) is its covariance. We treat \(g\) and \(F\) as constant.

Derivation: the two terms, step by step
  • With \(g\) and \(F\) constant, step \(i\) adds \(-\eta(g + \varepsilon_i)\). Summing \(k\) steps gives the displacement above.
  • Substituting it into \(\mathrm{KL} \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\theta^\top F \Delta\theta\) gives \(\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^2 (kg + \textstyle\sum_i \varepsilon_i)^\top F (kg + \textstyle\sum_i \varepsilon_i)\).
  • The signal–signal part is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^2 k^2\, g^\top F g = c_{\text{signal}}\,\eta^2 k^2\).
  • Each cross term contains a single \(\varepsilon_i\), whose mean is zero, so it vanishes in expectation.
  • In the noise–noise part, terms with \(i \ne j\) vanish because different mini-batches are independent. Each of the \(k\) terms with \(i = j\) has expectation \(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(F\Sigma)/B\), since a mini-batch of \(B\) responses has covariance \(\Sigma/B\). Together they give \(c_{\text{noise}}\,\eta^2 k/B\).

What each setting does. Batch size, learning rate, and staleness act on the two terms differently, and each has its own cost:

SettingNoise termSignal termCost
Larger batch \(B\)✅ shrinks as \(1/B\)❌ unchangedmore samples per step
Lower learning rate \(\eta\)✅ shrinks as \(\eta^2\)✅ shrinks as \(\eta^2\)less learning per step
Less staleness \(k\)✅ shrinks as \(k\)✅ shrinks as \(k^2\)more frequent sampler syncs

Late in a rollout the signal term dominates, so less staleness helps most and a larger batch least. The two terms also differ in value. Distance from noise is pure cost, because the trainer moves without learning, while distance from the signal is the price of learning. A larger batch removes only the first, and the other two settings shrink both.

Testing the formula. The table above says a larger batch shrinks only the noise term, so we can test the formula by changing the batch alone. If the KL were all noise, an 8× larger batch would cut it 8×. If it were all signal, the larger batch would not change it at all. We run one rollout at batch 128 and one at batch 1024, and Figure 10 plots the ratio of their KLs at every step.

The ratio starts near 6, so early in a rollout most of the KL is noise. By the last step it has fallen below 2, because the signal has piled up and a larger batch cannot remove it. The two-term formula, fitted on other runs, predicts the same decline (orange dashed curve).

A larger batch therefore helps most on fresh mini-batches, where the KL is small, and least on the stalest ones, where the KL is largest and SC breaks. The older the data, the more of the distance comes from learning itself, which no batch size removes.

4. What rescues SC

Section 2.3 showed that SC alone breaks once the trainer moves far from the sampler, that is, once the largest KL in a rollout grows large. We compare three ways to rescue it: changing the settings that set this distance, adding importance weights, and adding a KL penalty that pulls the trainer back.

4.1 Training settings: control the distance at the source

The most direct fix is to change the settings that set the distance. Section 3 showed how batch size, learning rate, and staleness each act on the noise and signal parts of the KL. We start from batch 32 at staleness 63, where SC collapses (Figure 7), and change one setting at a time (Figure 11). We compare staleness at batch 64, the smallest batch we ran at both staleness 63 and 15.

We find that a larger batch, a lower learning rate, and less staleness each rescue SC. The three settings remove different parts of the KL:

  • Larger batch. It averages out the noise. Going from batch 32 to 512 only halves the first-rollout KL, because about half of that KL comes from the learning signal itself. In later rollouts, when the KL is mostly noise, the larger batch helps much more.
  • Lower learning rate. Smaller steps keep the trainer closer. Cutting the learning rate to a third shrinks both the noise and the learning signal in each step, so it works whatever the KL is made of. The KL falls by more than an order of magnitude.
  • Less staleness. It is the main fix when the data is very old. On stale data, the learning signal piles up in the same direction at every step, so it grows with the square of staleness. We find that going from staleness 63 to 15 at batch 64 cuts the KL by a similar factor, and SC alone then trains to the end (Figure 11, right).

The two-term formula of Section 3 predicts these reductions well while the trainer stays close to the sampler. Late in a rollout at learning rate 0.03, the KL grows faster than the formula allows, so a lower learning rate or less staleness helps even more than predicted.

4.2 Importance weights: what they add on top of SC

Another option is importance weights, which correct the reweighting error that SC leaves. Vanilla IS needs no SC, as the starred run in Figure 4 shows. TIS does, because truncation brings drift back. The weights act differently on tokens with large and small ratios \(w = p/q\), and neither side rescues training where SC alone fails.

Large weights. When noise pushes the trainer toward tokens the sampler rarely picks, the ratios of those tokens grow, and only these rare samples, with their huge weights, could pull the trainer back. It is well known that untruncated weights can grow very large. Two findings from our runs are less obvious. First, the weights break vanilla IS even when the trainer stays close to the sampler. An fp8 sampler with a bf16 trainer adds a small rounding difference to every probability. Without staleness, vanilla IS still tracks on-policy, but at staleness 15 a single weight jumps to nearly 20,000 and the run collapses, while SC alone and SC + TIS train to the end (Figure 12).

Second, a small KL is no protection. At staleness 63 and batch 512, SC alone moves about 10× further from the sampler than vanilla IS in the first rollout, yet SC trains to the end and vanilla IS collapses in both seeds (Figure 13).

Small weights. Tokens the trainer already assigns less probability than the sampler have \(w < 1\). When IS pushes such a token down, its weight shrinks and the push weakens, while SC keeps pushing at full strength. By the end of the first rollout at staleness 63, 19–35% of tokens under SC alone have \(w\) below 0.5, against 3–5% under SC + TIS and 3% under vanilla IS, and the KL on the stalest mini-batch is 10–30× smaller with TIS. Giving SC this damping alone, by multiplying the terms with negative advantage by \(\min(1, w)\), has the same effect and lowers the first-rollout KL from 0.82 to 0.06 (Figure 14).

What TIS keeps and what it gives up. TIS truncates only above \(C\), so it keeps the damping on small weights and removes the risk from large ones. But truncation also removes the correction carried by large weights, and the sudden KL spikes of Section 2.3 come from that side. The damping slows the steady move away from the sampler, but it does not stop the spikes. Over whole runs, SC + TIS collapses in the same settings as SC alone, on 4B at batch 16 (Figure 8) and at staleness 63 with batch 32, and SC + \(\min(1, w)\) does not prevent failure either (Figure 15). Damping small weights shrinks the KL but does not replace fixing the source (Section 4.1).

4.3 A KL penalty: stability at a cost

The last option is a KL penalty. Instead of changing the source, it pulls the trainer back toward the sampler, like a spring. The spring pulls back every move, whether it comes from noise or from learning. A weak spring lets the trainer move far, and a strong one also holds back learning. A lower learning rate also shrinks both, but every mini-batch still counts. The spring holds the trainer in place after a few steps, so its update rests on fewer mini-batches and is noisier.

Derivation: the KL penalty as a spring

Near the sampler, the gradient of \(\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p_\theta)\) is about \(\beta F \Delta\theta\), and the displacement follows

\[ \Delta\theta_{k+1} = (I - \eta\beta F)\,\Delta\theta_k \;-\; \eta\,(g + \varepsilon_k). \]

Along a direction of curvature \(\lambda\), each step shrinks the existing displacement by a factor \(1 - \eta\beta\lambda\). After about \(1/(\eta\beta\lambda)\) steps, neither the signal nor the noise term of Section 3 grows any further, so the penalty limits how far the trainer can move within a rollout. It also means that after \(k\) steps, mini-batch \(j\) counts with weight \((1 - \eta\beta\lambda)^{k-j}\), so the older a mini-batch is, the less it counts. A lower learning rate weights every mini-batch equally, and for the same learning signal, equal weights leave the least noise.

Figure 16 compares the two where SC alone collapses, with the learning rate cut to a third. Since the KL grows with the square of the learning rate (Section 3), a third of the step should cut it about ninefold. It does at least that. The largest KL in a rollout falls from 0.7–3.5 to 0.03–0.16, and SC alone trains to the end.

Every penalty in Figure 16 stops the collapse, but only a tuned one matches a lower learning rate. Tinker's default (\(\beta = 0.01\)) leaves the KL high and ends about 6 points lower. A strong penalty (\(\beta = 0.3\)) keeps the KL smallest of all, yet ends about 10 points lower. Among the constant penalties, only \(\beta = 0.075\) matches the lower learning rate. The KL within a rollout shows the difference. Under the lower learning rate it keeps growing until the last mini-batch. Under a constant penalty it levels off, so the later mini-batches add little. A smaller KL does not by itself mean better training.

A penalty that decays with token age does no better. We also ran the schedule from Zhang (2026), which weakens the penalty as \(\beta_0/\sqrt{\text{age}}\) (orange in Figure 16). Decaying the penalty helps over a constant one of the same starting strength, and the best schedule, \(\beta_0 = 0.3\) with the exact KL, matches the lower learning rate. None beats it.

Why a lower learning rate matches a tuned penalty. Three things favor the learning rate. First, it keeps every mini-batch. By step \(k\), the spring shrinks the contribution of mini-batch \(j\) to \((1 - \eta\beta\lambda)^{k-j}\), so the update rests on the last few mini-batches and is noisier. A lower learning rate weights all 64 equally. Second, it comes with a rule. The KL scales with \(\eta^2\), so the KL you measure tells you how far to cut. A penalty acts through \(\eta\beta\lambda\), which depends on the curvature and on the number of steps in a rollout, so its strength has to be found by search. Our seven settings range from 12 points below the lower learning rate to level with it. Third, a penalty can overshoot. At \(\beta = 0.3\), the largest KL stays between 0.01 and 0.05, several times below that of the lower learning rate, and the run ends 10 points lower. The distance it removes is learning.

On 4B, the default penalty barely learns. With Tinker’s default penalty on DAPO-Math at batch 16 (purple in Figure 8), SC no longer collapses, but the training reward never trends up. Its final checkpoint scores 21% on AIME, close to the base model and far below the batch-128 runs (Figure 9). In our settings, a KL penalty trades the collapse for a lower end reward, and the price depends on \(\beta\).

5. A checklist for async RL

Figure 17 turns our results into a checklist, with one box for each step below.

1. Rule out setup bugs. These checks are almost free, and no correction survives a large mismatch (Fang & Khazi, 2025). Measure the KL at zero staleness. If it is clearly above zero, check the following.

  • Temperature. The trainer divides its logits by the same temperature the sampler used.
  • Top-p and top-k. If the sampler truncates its distribution, the trainer applies the same sampling mask. Otherwise, turn truncated sampling off.
  • MoE routing. Replay the sampler’s expert routing in the trainer.
  • Tokenization. Pass token IDs from the sampler to the trainer. Re-tokenizing the text can split it differently, and then the sampler’s log-probabilities no longer line up with the trainer’s tokens.

2. Turn on score centering. It removes drift, the error that amplifies itself and that no batch size averages away. If you also use importance sampling, truncate the weights.

3. Watch how far the trainer moves. The largest KL in each rollout is the signal to watch (Section 2.3). While it stays small, score centering alone is enough.

4. If it grows, fix it at the source. Lower the learning rate first, since it matches the best KL penalty with nothing to tune (Section 4.3). Use a larger batch while the distance is mostly noise, and reduce staleness when the data is very old (Section 4.1).

5. Add a KL penalty only when the source cannot change, and tune its strength. The default strength can stop learning (Section 4.3).

6. Conclusion

Score centering removes drift, the error that made off-policy training collapse at any batch size. What it leaves is distance, how far the trainer moves from the sampler before fresh data arrives. Part of that distance is noise, which a larger batch removes. The other part is learning itself, which only less staleness or smaller steps can shrink.

This split also explains why the two results we started from disagree. The score centering paper uses 512 responses per step, which keeps the noise small and the trainer close, so SC + TIS is stable. Zhang (2026) uses a few prompts per step on data tens to hundreds of steps old, so the trainer moves far and training collapses. Zhang’s SC + TIS also applies the truncated weight after centering, unlike ours and the SC paper’s, which lets part of the drift return. Our drift-free SC + TIS still collapses at small batches, so batch size and staleness alone explain the difference.

The split also points to what could come after SC. The levers it suggests need no new algorithm, because every RL run already has them. Staleness could follow the KL, with the sampler syncing early when the KL starts to climb, and teams that control how GPUs are split between training and inference could set that split the same way. Industrial runs already sit in a different regime. MiMo-V2.6 (LLM-Core Xiaomi, 2026) runs about 30 RL steps, each on 1,568 prompts × 16 responses, a batch large enough to keep the noise part of the distance small. When async RL training becomes unstable, it helps to first ask what broke it, whether noise, learning on old data, or a single extreme weight.

7. Open questions and related work

Several questions remain open.

  • Can staleness follow the KL instead of a fixed schedule? Staleness is usually fixed in advance for throughput. The largest KL in a rollout rises before SC collapses, and the last steps of a rollout add most of it. Syncing early whenever that KL grows too large would give up throughput only when training needs it.
  • How much does SC add once every method is tuned? Every method in our runs used the same batch size and learning rate, and a method without SC might do better at other settings. Untruncated IS, for example, collapses at learning rate 0.03 at staleness 7 and batch 64, but trains to the end at 0.015. The fairer test tunes TIS and a KL penalty on their own, without SC, and compares each one’s best run with the best run of SC.
  • Do the thresholds move under Adam or Muon? We use SGD so that a larger batch changes only the noise. Adam rescales every update, which ties the step size to the batch. Muon, which some large runs now use, orthogonalizes each update, so its step size depends even less on the gradient’s scale. MiMo-V2.6, for example, switches its hidden weights to a Muon variant before RL. The question is whether the split between noise and learning survives under either optimizer, and where SC alone then stops being enough.
Related work

Truncated importance sampling. Ionides (2008) introduces truncation with a ceiling \(\propto \sqrt{n}\). ACER and V-trace use fixed ceilings in deep RL. Yao et al. (2025) bring token-level TIS to LLM RL, and Liu et al. (2025) use it to train with 8-bit samplers. Later work finds that fixed ceilings mostly truncate low-confidence tokens (Yu et al., 2026) and that no single ceiling works throughout training (Zhou et al., 2026).

Score centering and KL penalties. Our drift decomposition follows Marek & Ryabinin (2026). Zhang (2026) bounds the error left by SC with the KL between the trainer and the sampler, observes that SC does not bound this KL, and adds a KL penalty that decays with staleness. To raise staleness, Zhang cuts each rollout into smaller steps, which also shrinks the batch, so the noise and signal terms grow together. Thinking Machines' StableReinforce uses REINFORCE + SC + a fixed-coefficient KL penalty (Marek, 2026). PRIME-RL’s IPO masks tokens whose probability difference exceeds a threshold, on both sides. In our framework, all of these limit the KL between the trainer and the sampler through the loss.

Trust regions. TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015) and PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) keep each update near the policy that collected the data, because the surrogate objective is only reliable there. We split that distance into a noise part and a signal part. A trust region, like a KL penalty, does not tell them apart.

Batch, noise, and KL. McCandlish et al. (2018) define the critical batch size via the gradient noise scale. Our two-term decomposition is its off-policy counterpart. Smith & Le (2018) describe SGD noise with a temperature \(\propto \eta/B\). The scaling rule matters. Linear scaling (Goyal et al., 2017) increases noise with the batch, while square-root scaling (Malladi et al., 2022) keeps it constant.
