Drift and Distance: When Is Score Centering Enough in Async RL?

October 27 · 2026

In asynchronous RL for LLMs, the sampler generates responses while the trainer updates the policy. The trainer therefore learns from data produced by an older policy, a gap called staleness. Even without staleness, differences between the two sides make training off-policy. Low-precision samplers (e.g., fp8) introduce rounding errors, and mismatched sampling configurations (temperature, top-p, top-k, MoE routing) can destabilize training even when the two sides are fully synchronized (Fang & Khazi, 2025).

Score centering (SC) removes the most harmful part of this error, a systematic push that we call drift (Marek & Ryabinin, 2026). Do we need anything beyond SC? The SC paper pairs SC with truncated importance sampling (TIS) and trains stably. Zhang (2026) finds that the same combination collapses and adds a KL penalty, as does Thinking Machines' StableReinforce. Why does the same recipe work in one setup and fail in another?

Our findings. Score centering keeps training stable across most of our settings. However, it can still collapse in extreme cases. We trace these failures to their causes and test which fixes rescue training, from auxiliary losses such as TIS and a KL penalty to the training settings themselves. We find that batch size and learning rate decide how far the trainer moves away from the sampler, and that larger batches can tolerate staler data. Sometimes, adjusting batch size and learning rate works better than adding a loss. We hope these findings help explain how the train–inference mismatch changes training dynamics.

1. Background and setup

This section introduces the two errors of off-policy data and the corrections that target them, then describes how we test when SC alone is enough.

1.1 Two errors

Off-policy data introduces two kinds of error (Marek & Ryabinin, 2026; Zhang, 2026), shown in Figure 1. Below, \(p\) is the trainer's policy and \(q\) the sampler's.

Drift. Under on-policy training, the expected policy gradient contains only the learning signal: it raises the probability of high-advantage tokens. When the data comes from the sampler instead, the expected gradient picks up an extra term that does not depend on which token is better. Under a softmax parameterization, this term points along \(q - p\), scaled by the mean advantage at the prefix.

Reweighting error. The sampler draws tokens from its own distribution, but the trainer needs feedback weighted by its own. If the trainer assigns 10% to a token the sampler assigns 1%, that token appears 10× less often than the trainer needs. Weighting it by 10 would restore the balance.

1.2 Three corrections

Three corrections are in common use. Score centering targets drift, importance sampling targets reweighting error, and a KL penalty keeps the trainer near the sampler.

  • Score centering (SC) removes drift without introducing weights. It recenters each token’s gradient around its average under the sampler. That average is what drift adds, so subtracting it cancels drift. The reweighting error remains.
  • Importance sampling (IS) corrects reweighting error by weighting each token by the ratio of the trainer’s probability to the sampler’s. When the two policies diverge, a few weights can become extreme and dominate an entire update. The standard remedy is truncated importance sampling (TIS), which truncates the weights at a ceiling \(C\) (Yao et al., 2025).
  • A KL penalty pulls the trainer back toward the sampler with strength \(\beta\).

Figure 2 compares the options by the coefficient each puts on the drift term. Without correction, the coefficient is the mean advantage at the prefix, which can have either sign. A positive one pulls the trainer toward the sampler and stops once the gap closes. A negative one pushes the trainer away, harder the further it has gone, and this feedback makes drift the most dangerous error. SC sets the coefficient to zero, and a KL penalty on top of SC sets it to \(+\beta\), which always pulls toward the sampler (Zhang, 2026).

1.3 When is SC enough?

So far we have split off-policy error into drift and reweighting error (Section 1.1) and seen that SC removes the drift (Section 1.2). Is SC then enough on its own? We expect the answer to depend on distance: how far the trainer moves from the sampler before fresh data arrives, measured with KL. This KL bounds the reweighting error that SC leaves (Zhang, 2026). Close to the sampler, stale data is still a good guide. Far from it, SC alone may fail.

To find where this boundary lies, we vary staleness and batch size, the two settings that differ most between the setups we started from, and compare SC alone with SC plus importance sampling or a KL penalty.

Optimizer. We use plain SGD rather than Adam. Adam normalizes each parameter’s update, so changing the batch size would change the effective step size as well as the noise. With SGD and the loss averaged over the batch, only the noise changes.

Full 0.8B runs all take 640 steps, so a larger batch sees more samples, and we compare batch sizes on stability only.

2. SC stabilizes training, but not always

We first test SC and importance sampling side by side in one setting, then make the batch larger, and then make the data older and the batch smaller.

2.1 Removing drift: SC and importance sampling, on and off

Section 1.2 described what each correction targets. Here we test them together in one setup, with batch 64 and a maximum staleness of 15. Figure 4 runs three strategies, no importance sampling (no IS), untruncated importance sampling (vanilla IS), and truncated importance sampling (TIS, ceiling \(C = 2\)), each with and without SC.

Without SC, all three runs collapse. No IS keeps all of the drift, TIS truncates part of the correction so drift returns, and vanilla IS removes drift only on average and dies from extreme weights.

With SC, SC alone and SC + TIS train to the end, but vanilla IS still collapses. SC removes drift, so what remains is the risk from the weights themselves. Truncation bounds them, but untruncated weights can still grow extreme with SC, a failure we return to in Section 4.3.

How the six runs differ

All six can be written in a single formula. For each token, the update direction is:

\[ \hat g = A \Big( w(x)\,\nabla\log p(x) \;-\; \mathbb{E}_{x'\sim q}\big[w(x')\,\nabla\log p(x')\big] \Big), \]

where \(x\) is the sampled token, \(A\) is its advantage, and the expectation runs over all candidate tokens at that position under the sampler \(q\). The weight \(w\) depends on the IS variant: \(w = 1\) for no IS, \(w = p(x)/q(x)\) for vanilla IS (the ratio \(\pi_{\text{train}}/\pi_{\text{rollout}}\)), and \(w = \min(p(x)/q(x),\, C)\) for TIS. The second term inside the parentheses is SC, the sampler average that cancels drift (Section 1.2). Without SC, only the first term remains.

Vanilla IS removes drift in expectation (\(\mathbb{E}_q[w\nabla\log p] = \mathbb{E}_p[\nabla\log p] = 0\)), and vanilla IS + SC is the same estimator as vanilla IS: its expected weighted score is already zero, so the SC term vanishes.

In this setting, SC alone is enough.

2.2 Large batches: SC alone keeps up with on-policy training

Batch 64 was small enough for vanilla IS to collapse. We now raise the batch to 512 at the same staleness and ask whether that keeps all three methods stable (Figure 5).

The left two panels of Figure 5 show that a larger batch is enough to stabilize all three methods. At batch 512, they all keep up with on-policy to the end, while at batch 64 the curves are noisy and vanilla IS collapses. The next subsection shows the same pattern on 4B at batch 128.

The larger batch works by shrinking the distance. An 8× larger batch lowers the median KL about 8×, and truncation then almost never triggers. Section 3 explains why the drop is this close to the batch ratio.

This works only because drift is gone. Without any correction, batch 512 collapses within the first rollout, just as batch 64 does, because drift is a systematic push that no batch size averages away. Whether a tuned TIS or KL penalty without SC also works is still open.

2.3 High staleness and small batches: SC alone can break

Next we move to the opposite regime, with older data and smaller batches. SC has no weights to blow up, but it can still fail when the trainer moves far. We run SC alone at staleness 63 with batches of 32, 64, and 512.

The left panel of Figure 6 shows that SC alone keeps up with on-policy at batch 512 but breaks down at smaller batches. Even the batch-64 seed that survives ends 14 points below its best. In the right panel, the smaller the batch, the larger the KL on the stalest mini-batch, about 1.5 at batch 32 against 0.1 to 0.3 at batch 512.

We repeat the test on 4B with the harder DAPO-Math-17k at staleness 15, which also lets us compare the three methods.

The 4B runs in Figure 7 fail the same way at small batch. At batch 16, all three methods collapse, and each collapse starts with a sudden spike in the KL to the sampler. At batch 128, all three keep up with on-policy and roughly double the base model’s AIME scores. The differences among them are within sampling error (see the evaluation below).

In both models, each failure comes with a large KL to the sampler. Section 3 looks at where that distance comes from.

3. Why SC fails: how the trainer moves away from the sampler

SC removes drift but does not limit how far the trainer moves on stale data. To see where that distance comes from, we split the KL within a rollout into a part from noise and a part from the learning signal, then test the split by changing the batch alone.

Definition. Within each rollout, after the sampler generates data, the trainer updates for \(k\) consecutive steps on that data. We measure the divergence with the per-token KL, \(\mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p_k)\), where \(q\) is the sampler's policy and \(p_k\) is the trainer's policy after \(k\) steps. We estimate it on the sampled tokens as the mean of \(w - 1 - \log w\), with \(w = p/q\) (Schulman, 2020). At the start of a rollout the KL is near zero, and it grows with each trainer step.

Signal and noise. Each step's gradient has two parts. The signal is the common direction all batches agree on. The noise is the random deviation specific to the current batch, with zero mean and variance proportional to \(1/B\). After \(k\) steps, the parameter displacement is:

\[ \theta_k - \theta_0 = -\eta\Big(\underbrace{k\,g}_{\text{signal: grows like } k} + \underbrace{\varepsilon_1 + \cdots + \varepsilon_k}_{\text{noise: grows like } \sqrt{k}}\Big). \]

Here \(\theta_0\) is the parameter at the start of the rollout, \(\theta_k\) the parameter after \(k\) steps, \(\eta\) the learning rate, \(g\) the signal (the expected gradient), and \(\varepsilon_i\) the noise at step \(i\). The signal accumulates in the same direction, so \(k\) steps give \(k\) steps of displacement. The noise is random across steps and partly cancels, so \(k\) steps give only about \(\sqrt{k}\).

KL is approximately a quadratic form of the displacement, \(\mathrm{KL} \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\theta^\top F \Delta\theta\), where \(F\) is the Fisher information matrix and measures how sensitive the policy is along each direction. Substituting the displacement and taking the expectation, the cross terms vanish and the KL splits into two terms:

\[ \mathrm{KL}_k \;\approx\; c_{\text{noise}}\,\frac{\eta^2 k}{B} \;+\; c_{\text{signal}}\,\eta^2 k^2. \]

Here \(c_{\text{signal}} = \tfrac{1}{2} g^\top F g\) is the squared length of the signal, weighted by sensitivity. Likewise, \(c_{\text{noise}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(F\Sigma)\) is the gradient variance of a single response, weighted the same way, where \(\Sigma\) is its covariance. We treat \(g\) and \(F\) as constant.

What each setting does. Batch size, learning rate, and staleness act on the two terms differently, and each has its own cost:

SettingNoise termSignal termCost
Larger batch \(B\)✅ shrinks as \(1/B\)❌ unchangedmore samples per step
Lower learning rate \(\eta\)✅ shrinks as \(\eta^2\)✅ shrinks as \(\eta^2\)less learning per step
Less staleness \(k\)✅ shrinks as \(k\)✅ shrinks as \(k^2\)more frequent sampler syncs

Late in a rollout the signal term dominates, so less staleness helps most and a larger batch least. The two terms also differ in value. Distance from noise is pure cost, because the trainer moves without learning, while distance from the signal is the price of learning. A larger batch removes only the first, and the other two settings shrink both.

Testing the formula. The table above says a larger batch shrinks only the noise term, so we can test the formula by changing the batch alone. If the KL were all noise, an 8× larger batch would cut it 8×. If it were all signal, the larger batch would not change it at all. We run one rollout at batch 128 and one at batch 1024, and Figure 9 plots the ratio of their KLs at every step.

The ratio starts near 6, so early in a rollout most of the KL is noise. By the last step it has fallen below 2, because the signal has piled up and a larger batch cannot remove it. The two-term formula, fitted on other runs, predicts the same decline (orange dashed curve).

A larger batch therefore helps most on fresh mini-batches, where the KL is small, and least on the stalest ones, where the KL is largest and SC breaks. The older the data, the more of the distance comes from learning itself, which no batch size removes.

4. What rescues SC

Section 2.3 showed that SC alone breaks once the trainer moves far from the sampler, that is, once the KL on the stalest mini-batch grows large. We compare three ways to rescue it: changing the settings that set this distance, adding a KL penalty that pulls the trainer back, and adding importance weights.

4.1 Training settings: control the distance at the source

The most direct fix is to change the settings that set the distance. Section 3 showed how batch size, learning rate, and staleness each act on the noise and signal parts of the KL. We start from batch 32 at staleness 63, where SC collapses (Figure 6), and change one setting at a time (Figure 10). We compare staleness at batch 64, the smallest batch we ran at both staleness 63 and 15.

We find that both a larger batch and a lower learning rate rescue SC, and so does less staleness. The three settings remove different parts of the KL:

  • Larger batch. It averages out the noise. Going from batch 32 to 512 only halves the first-rollout KL, because about half of that KL comes from the learning signal itself. In later rollouts, when the KL is mostly noise, the larger batch helps much more.
  • Lower learning rate. Smaller steps keep the trainer closer. Cutting the learning rate to a third shrinks both the noise and the learning signal in each step, so it works whatever the KL is made of. The KL falls by more than an order of magnitude.
  • Less staleness. It is the main fix when the data is very old. On stale data, the learning signal piles up in the same direction at every step, so it grows with the square of staleness. We find that going from staleness 63 to 15 at batch 64 cuts the KL by a similar factor, and SC alone then trains to the end (Figure 10, right).

The two-term formula of Section 3 predicts these reductions well while the trainer stays close to the sampler. Late in a rollout at learning rate 0.03, the KL grows faster than the formula allows, so a lower learning rate or less staleness helps even more than predicted.

So the right setting depends on what the KL is made of. Noise calls for a larger batch, the learning signal for less staleness, and a lower learning rate works on both.

4.2 A KL penalty: stability at a cost

Instead of changing the source, a KL penalty pulls the trainer back toward the sampler, like a spring. The spring pulls back every move, whether it comes from noise or from learning. A weak spring lets the trainer move far, and a strong one also holds back learning. A lower learning rate also shrinks both, but every mini-batch still counts. The spring holds the trainer in place after a few steps, so its update rests on fewer mini-batches and is noisier. Figure 11 compares the two where SC alone collapses, with the learning rate cut to a third.

Derivation: the KL penalty as a spring

Near the sampler, the gradient of \(\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p_\theta)\) is about \(\beta F \Delta\theta\), and the displacement follows

\[ \Delta\theta_{k+1} = (I - \eta\beta F)\,\Delta\theta_k \;-\; \eta\,(g + \varepsilon_k). \]

Along a direction of curvature \(\lambda\), each step shrinks the existing displacement by a factor \(1 - \eta\beta\lambda\). After about \(1/(\eta\beta\lambda)\) steps, neither the signal nor the noise term of Section 3 grows any further, so the penalty limits how far the trainer can move within a rollout. It also means that after \(k\) steps, mini-batch \(j\) counts with weight \((1 - \eta\beta\lambda)^{k-j}\), so the older a mini-batch is, the less it counts. A lower learning rate weights every mini-batch equally, and for the same learning signal, equal weights leave the least noise.

Every penalty in Figure 11 stops the collapse, but only a tuned one matches a lower learning rate. Tinker's default (\(\beta = 0.01\)) leaves the KL high and ends about 6 points lower. A strong penalty (\(\beta = 0.3\)) keeps the KL smallest of all, yet ends about 10 points lower. Only \(\beta = 0.075\) matches the lower learning rate. The KL within a rollout shows the difference. Under the lower learning rate it keeps growing until the last mini-batch. Under a constant penalty it levels off, so the later mini-batches add little. A smaller KL does not by itself mean better training.

A penalty that decays with token age does no better. We also ran the schedule from Zhang (2026), which weakens the penalty as \(\beta_0/\sqrt{\text{age}}\). Decaying the penalty helps over a constant one of the same starting strength, but no schedule beats the lower learning rate.

End reward of each penalty schedule

On 4B, the default penalty barely learns. With Tinker’s default penalty on DAPO-Math at batch 16 (purple in Figure 7), SC no longer collapses, but the training reward never trends up. Its final checkpoint scores 21% on AIME, close to the base model and far below the batch-128 runs (Figure 8). In our settings, a KL penalty trades the collapse for a lower end reward, and the price depends on \(\beta\).

4.3 Importance weights: a risk of their own

The last option is to add importance weights, which correct the reweighting error that SC leaves. Vanilla IS applies each weight in full, and TIS truncates it at \(C\). In our runs, neither rescues SC. Vanilla IS adds a failure of its own, and TIS behaves like SC alone.

Vanilla IS. It multiplies each weight into the gradient, so a few large weights can dominate an update. With small batches this feeds on itself. A noisy step moves the trainer away from the sampler, some ratios \(p/q\) grow, and the next update, scaled by those ratios, moves it further. The right panel of Figure 5 shows the largest ratio rising with the KL, and we think this loop is why vanilla IS collapses at batch 64 but not at batch 512. SC alone sees similar ratios but never multiplies them into the gradient.

The weights can break vanilla IS even when the trainer stays close to the sampler. An fp8 sampler with a bf16 trainer adds a small rounding difference to every probability. Without staleness, vanilla IS still tracks on-policy, but at staleness 15 a single weight jumps to nearly 20,000 and the run collapses (Figure 13). SC alone and SC + TIS train to the end.

The contrast is sharpest at staleness 63 and batch 512. In the first rollout, SC alone moves about 10× further from the sampler than vanilla IS, yet SC trains to the end and vanilla IS collapses in both seeds (Figure 14). For vanilla IS, a small KL is no protection.

TIS. Truncation removes the risk from extreme weights, and SC + TIS survives the fp8 sampler. But it collapses in the same settings as SC alone, on 4B at batch 16 (Figure 7) and at staleness 63 with batch 32. When noise pushes the trainer toward tokens the sampler rarely picks, IS could pull it back only through samples of those tokens, which are rare and carry huge weights. Vanilla IS applies these weights and blows up first. TIS truncates them, so it behaves like SC alone and its KL spikes about as often. Which of the two collapses first is a matter of chance.

Supplement: correcting SC’s low end

IS and SC also differ on the low side. When IS pushes a token's probability down, its weight shrinks and the push weakens, while SC keeps pushing. This comes from the weight itself. TIS only truncates above \(C\), so it does the same. By the end of the first rollout at staleness 63, 21% of tokens under SC have a ratio \(p/q\) below 0.5, against 3% under vanilla IS. Limiting how far SC pushes these tokens down is a natural fix to try.

Giving SC the same low-side limit means multiplying the terms with negative advantage by \(\min(1, w)\).

Figure 15 shows that this lowers SC’s first-rollout KL from 0.82 to 0.06, close to vanilla IS.

Over whole runs, though, it does not prevent failure (Figure 16). It stops SC from slowly moving away from the sampler, but not the sudden KL spikes of Section 2.3. Correcting the low end shrinks the KL but does not replace fixing the source (Section 4.1).

5. What this means in practice

Figure 17 summarizes our SGD results as a decision flow.

  1. Always turn on score centering. It removes drift, the error that amplifies itself and that no batch size averages away. If you also use importance sampling, truncate the weights.
  2. Ask how far the trainer moves, and fix that at the source. Lower the learning rate, use a larger batch while the gap is mostly noise, or reduce staleness when the data is very old.
  3. Extra losses on top of SC (TIS, a KL penalty) only patch the problem. Use them when the source cannot change.

6. Conclusion

Our runs suggest why the two results we started from disagree. Once drift is removed, SC alone is enough as long as the trainer stays close to the sampler, and it fails when the trainer moves far. The score centering paper uses 512 responses per step, which keeps the noise small and the trainer close, so SC + TIS is stable. Zhang (2026) uses a few prompts per step on data tens to hundreds of steps old, so the trainer moves far and training collapses. Note that Zhang’s SC + TIS applies the truncated weight after centering, unlike ours and the SC paper’s, so part of the drift may return. Our drift-free SC + TIS still collapses at small batches, so batch size and staleness alone can explain the difference.

The distance between the trainer and the sampler has two sources with different costs. Distance from noise is pure cost, and a larger batch removes it. Distance from the signal is the price of learning, and every way to reduce it, including a KL penalty, gives up some learning or throughput. When async RL training becomes unstable, it helps to first ask what broke it, whether noise, learning on old data, or a single extreme weight.

7. Open questions and related work

We close with the questions our runs leave open and with related work.

Open questions
  • Does this hold under Adam? Production training uses Adam, whose normalization ties the step size to the batch. A larger batch should still mean less noise, but the thresholds may move.
  • Is score centering enough under stronger mismatch? The score centering paper also tests an fp8 KV cache, which changed little in our model (Section 4.3). Models with more attention layers, or FP4 caches, may add much more mismatch.
  • What about long responses? SC and IS correct each token given its prefix, but the prefix itself comes from the sampler. This is like correcting actions but not states in off-policy actor-critic (Degris et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2019), and the prefix mismatch grows with response length.
  • Is SC needed once everything else is tuned? We compare methods at the same settings. Whether TIS or a KL penalty without SC, each with its own best batch size and learning rate, can match SC remains open.
Related work

Truncated importance sampling. Ionides (2008) introduces truncation with a ceiling \(\propto \sqrt{n}\). ACER and V-trace use fixed ceilings in deep RL. Yao et al. (2025) bring token-level TIS to LLM RL, and Liu et al. (2025) use it to train with 8-bit samplers. Later work finds that fixed ceilings mostly truncate low-confidence tokens (Yu et al., 2026) and that no single ceiling works throughout training (Zhou et al., 2026).

Score centering and KL penalties. Our drift decomposition follows Marek & Ryabinin (2026). Zhang (2026) bounds the error left by SC with the KL between the trainer and the sampler, observes that SC does not bound this KL, and adds a KL penalty that decays with staleness. To raise staleness, Zhang cuts each rollout into smaller steps, which also shrinks the batch, so the noise and signal terms grow together. Thinking Machines' StableReinforce uses REINFORCE + SC + a fixed-coefficient KL penalty (Marek, 2026). PRIME-RL’s IPO masks tokens whose probability difference exceeds a threshold, on both sides. In our framework, all of these limit the KL between the trainer and the sampler through the loss.

Trust regions. TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015) and PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) keep each update near the policy that collected the data, because the surrogate objective is only reliable there. We split that distance into a noise part and a signal part. A trust region, like a KL penalty, does not tell them apart.

Batch, noise, and KL. McCandlish et al. (2018) define the critical batch size via the gradient noise scale. Our two-term decomposition is its off-policy counterpart. Smith & Le (2018) describe SGD noise with a temperature \(\propto \eta/B\). The scaling rule matters. Linear scaling (Goyal et al., 2017) increases noise with the batch, while square-root scaling (Malladi et al., 2022) keeps it constant.
